Along most of Hatteras Island, a line of dunes stands between the ocean and {% include hatteras/term.liquid id="nc-12" %}. After storms, sand washed onto the road is pushed back onto the dunes, and low or broken dunes are rebuilt.
A tall, continuous dune keeps small and medium storms off the road and out of the villages.
It also stops {% include hatteras/term.liquid id="overwash" text="overwash" %}, the natural process that carries sand onto the island and lets it build upward as the sea rises.
An island held behind a fixed {% include hatteras/term.liquid id="dune-line" text="dune line" %} can grow lower and narrower over time, compared with one that is free to roll landward.

## A dune line built by people

Before the dunes were built, Hatteras was closer to a natural barrier island. Its dunes were low and broken, and in big storms the water ran between them and across the island, carrying sand into the marshes behind.
That overwash is how the island built itself up and moved slowly westward as the sea rose.
It also meant no road could last: storms and overwash kept a permanent road off the Outer Banks until the 1930s.

In the 1930s it was decided to build a protective line of dunes between the planned road and the beach.
Between 1936 and 1940, Civilian Conservation Corps and Works Progress Administration crews directed by the National Park Service, put up almost 3 million feet of sand fencing, which trapped blowing sand into a continuous barrier dune along the Outer Banks.
The Park Service added to it in the late 1950s and rebuilt dunes after the 1962 Ash Wednesday storm, then stopped maintaining the dune line in the early 1970s; today dunes in front of NC-12 are rebuilt after storms.

The built dune changed the island. Roads, houses and businesses went up right behind it, so the line where people built became the line that had to be defended.
The beach in front of the dune, with nowhere to go, grew narrower and steeper: by 1972, along many stretches of Hatteras Island, it had shrunk to about 150 feet (45 m) or almost disappeared.

<summary>Go deeper: held in place, or free to roll over (an example from Maryland)</summary>

Ocean City and Assateague Island, on the Maryland coast just north of Virginia, show the difference side by side.
Ocean City is built up, and its beach is nourished to hold the shoreline where it is.
Assateague, across the inlet, is a national seashore left to behave naturally: storms wash over its low dunes and carry sand across the island into the bay behind.
That overwash is how a barrier island rolls landward as the sea rises, so Assateague has moved landward while Ocean City has stayed put.
The jetties built in 1934–35 to hold Ocean City Inlet open also cut Assateague off from the sand drifting south, which sped its retreat.

Hatteras sits between the two. Its villages and road are held in place much like Ocean City, behind a built dune line that keeps most overwash off the island.

<details class="hat-deeper" markdown="1">
<summary>Go deeper: how the model represents dune management</summary>

The model follows the same rules every year:

- **Along the road**, whenever a storm lowers the dune in front of NC-12 below a minimum height, the dune is rebuilt to a set design height. Overwash sand that lands on the road is pushed back onto the dune.
- **In the villages** (Buxton, Avon, and Salvo–Waves–Rodanthe), part of the overwash that lands among houses is removed, as people clear streets and yards. The model removes 40%, the residential end of the range measured by Rogers and others (2015).
- **Where no road or village needs protecting**, such as Cape Point and the stretch where the Jug Handle Bridge now carries the road over the {% include hatteras/term.liquid id="sound" text="sound" %}, dunes are left to change naturally.

</details>
