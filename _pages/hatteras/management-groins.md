A groin is a wall built out across the beach into the surf.
It catches sand moving along the shore, so the beach on the side the sand comes from grows wider.
The beach on the other side is cut off from that sand and often erodes faster.

## A short history

In 1969–70 the US Navy built three groins at Buxton, about 1,670 feet of them in all, to protect its Naval Facility at Cape Hatteras: two north of where the Cape Hatteras Lighthouse then stood and one just south of it. They also shielded the lighthouse. The groins were lengthened in 1980–82.
They were built before North Carolina's coastal management law, and before the state banned new hard structures on its ocean beaches.

The groins trapped sand drifting south along the shore, and the beach south of them, cut off from it, was set back: a 2026 draft report for the state's Coastal Resources Commission describes a landward offset of the shoreline downdrift of the groins.
The shore kept retreating toward the lighthouse, and in the summer of 1999 the lighthouse itself was moved 2,900 feet (880 m) to the southwest.
The 2017 and 2022 Buxton nourishment projects start at the southern groin and run north.

## The repair under way

Today the two northern groins are completely submerged on most days, and the southern one (groin 3 in the photos) has deteriorated.
Dare County is repairing it, to trap more sand and keep the beach to its north wide enough to protect NC-12 through Buxton.
The plan restores 640 feet of the groin, with stone armour on its seaward 340 feet. The National Park Service took public comments in August and September 2025, and in February 2026 the state ruled the work a repair of an existing structure, which the hard-structure ban allows.
According to local news, the contract was awarded in June 2026, with the work due to be substantially complete by 15 December 2026.

## Why North Carolina banned hard structures

In January 1985 the state's Coastal Resources Commission banned permanent erosion-control structures on the ocean shoreline, including groins, jetties, breakwaters and seawalls.
Its rule says they "may cause significant adverse impacts on the value and enjoyment of adjacent properties or public access to and use of the ocean beach".
In 2003 the ban was written into state law: "No person shall construct a permanent erosion control structure in an ocean shoreline."

In 2011 the legislature made an exception for terminal groins, built at the end of an island beside an inlet. Up to four were allowed at first, and up to seven today. Two have been built: at Bald Head Island (2015) and Ocean Isle Beach (2022).
Older structures such as the Buxton groins remain, and can be repaired.

## What groins and jetties have done elsewhere

Groins trap sand on the side it comes from, so the beach beyond them is starved of sand.

- **Folly Beach, South Carolina.** The Charleston Harbor jetties, the larger cousins of groins, were built in 1898. A 1987 Army Corps of Engineers study found that about 57% of the erosion of Folly Beach, just south of them, was due to the jetties and the harbour channel.
- **Westhampton Beach, New York.** A field of 15 groins built on Long Island trapped an estimated five million cubic yards of sand, starving the beach to the west, which breached in a storm in December 1992.
